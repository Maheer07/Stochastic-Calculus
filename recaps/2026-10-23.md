# Week 1 Recap — 2026-10-23 (Days 1–4)

Note: Week 1 had four lessons (Tue–Fri), because the course started on Tuesday.

## This week in one paragraph
We started with what "known at time t" means (Day 1). Then we described where a stock ends up: ln S_T is normal, so S_T is lognormal, and its median sits below its mean because of volatility drag (Day 2). Next we forecast "given what we know now". That gave us the fair-game (martingale) idea and discounting, and a preview of a special probability p* that makes the discounted stock fair (Day 3). Finally, we priced a call with **no probabilities at all**, by building a riskless portfolio of Δ shares and a short call, and found the same price that p* gave (Day 4). Monday explains why those two answers always agree.

## Day by day
**Day 1 — Information up to time t** (Tue)
- A decision at time t can only use what's known at time t. Every hedge ratio later in the course obeys this.
- The atoms and σ-algebra bookkeeping were optional scaffolding (the course follows Hull from Day 2).
- Python idea: enumerate every path of a 3-step coin-toss tree and sum probabilities.

**Day 2 — Normal & lognormal; continuous compounding** (Wed)
- Continuous compounding: growth e^{rT}, discounting e^{−rT}.
- Log returns add up (ln(ab) = ln a + ln b); simple returns don't.
- ln S_T ~ φ[ln S₀ + (μ − σ²/2)T, σ²T], so S_T is lognormal.
- Mean S₀e^{μT} > median S₀e^{(μ−σ²/2)T}: volatility drag.
- Python idea: simulate S_T = S₀·exp((μ − σ²/2)T + σ√T·ε) with 100,000 draws of ε.

**Day 3 — Expectations given today's information, martingales, discounting** (Thu)
- E_t[X] = best forecast of X using what's known at t. What's known comes out; a forecast of a forecast is today's forecast.
- Martingale: E_t[X_T] = X_t. A fair ±$1 game is one; X_n² is not (X_n² − n is).
- Under the real p the discounted stock drifts up. Under p* = (e^{rΔt} − d)/(u − d) it's fair.
- Python idea: compute one-step forecasts and solve for p*.

**Day 4 — One-step binomial tree, riskless portfolio, Δ** (Fri)
- Long Δ shares, short 1 option. Pick Δ = (f_u − f_d)/(S₀u − S₀d) so the payoff is the same in both states.
- A riskless portfolio earns r, so price = S₀Δ − PV(riskless payoff). Call: Δ = 0.5, price 5.448.
- Any other price gives an arbitrage. The real probability p never enters.
- Python idea: a `one_step()` pricer returning Δ and the price.

## Formula sheet
- Continuous compounding: A = P·e^{rT}; present value = X·e^{−rT}
- Log return: ln(S_T/S₀); log returns add across periods
- ln S_T ~ φ[ln S₀ + (μ − σ²/2)T, σ²T]
- E[S_T] = S₀e^{μT}; Var(S_T) = S₀²e^{2μT}(e^{σ²T} − 1); median = S₀e^{(μ−σ²/2)T}
- Tower rule: E₀[E₁[X]] = E₀[X]; martingale: E_t[X_T] = X_t
- Risk-neutral probability (one step): p = (e^{rΔt} − d)/(u − d)
- Δ = (f_u − f_d)/(S₀u − S₀d)
- Put–call parity: c − p = S₀ − Ke^{−rT}

## Solutions

**WC1: B (0.531).** e^{rT} = e^{0.0125} = 1.01258; u = 1.2, d = 0.8; p = (1.01258 − 0.8)/(1.2 − 0.8) = **0.5314**. A assumes 50/50. C has no basis here. D is 1 − p.

**WC2.** (a) Put payoffs: 0 (up), 10 (down). Δ = (0 − 10)/(60 − 40) = −0.5. With Δ shares and short 1 put: up −30 − 0 = −30; down −20 − 10 = −30, so it's riskless. PV = −30e^{−0.0125} = −29.627. Cost today −25 − f = −29.627 → **f = 4.627**. (b) e^{−0.0125} × (1 − 0.5314) × 10 = 0.98758 × 4.6855 = **4.627** ✓.

**WC3.** Seed 42, n = 100,000: estimate ≈ **9.39**, standard error ≈ **0.045**. The exact Black–Scholes value is **9.413** (d₁ = 0.25, d₂ = 0.05), well within about one standard error. Setting μ = r is the "risk-neutral world" we formalise on Monday.
