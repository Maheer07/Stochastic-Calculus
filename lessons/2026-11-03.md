### Recall
A Wiener process z has Δz = ε√Δt and independent changes, so z(T) − z(0) ~ φ(0, T). On average it doesn't go anywhere (zero drift), and its variance is exactly T. Real variables trend and have their own volatility. Today we add both.

### Today's concept
*Hull: "Wiener Processes and Itô's Lemma" — "Generalized Wiener Process" and "Itô Process".*

**Generalized Wiener process.**
  **dx = a dt + b dz**
- **a dt** is the *drift*: x moves a per unit time on average.
- **b dz** is the *noise*: a Wiener process scaled by b.
- Without the noise, dx = a dt gives x = x₀ + at, a straight line (dx/dt = a is an ordinary derivative).

In discrete time: **Δx = aΔt + bε√Δt**, so over T:
  x(T) − x(0) ~ **φ(aT, b²T)** — mean aT, variance b²T, st. dev. b√T.

**Worked example.** x₀ = 100, a = 8 per year, b = 15 per year.
- After 1 year: x ~ φ(108, 225), st. dev. 15.
- After 3 months: x ~ φ(102, 56.25), st. dev. 15√0.25 = **7.5**.

**Drift vs noise over time.** The drift grows like T, but the noise only like √T. So the drift-to-noise ratio is aT/(b√T) = (a/b)√T. Here that's **0.03 over a day, 0.27 over 3 months, 0.53 over 1 year, 1.07 over 4 years**. Over short horizons noise swamps the drift. That's why a stock's daily moves look almost pure noise even when it trends upward over years.

**Itô process.** Let the drift and noise depend on where x is and when:
  **dx = a(x, t) dt + b(x, t) dz**.
Over a small step, Δx ≈ a(x, t)Δt + b(x, t)ε√Δt, using the values of a and b at the *start* of the step. That's exactly how you simulate it (step by step, updating x each time).

Two examples:
- **Stock price (tomorrow):** a = μS, b = σS, so both drift and noise grow with the price.
- **Mean reversion:** a = κ(θ − x) pulls x back towards a level θ. Used for interest rates in Module H.

**Code** (generalized Wiener process; check the formulas):
```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 252; dt = 1/n; x0, a, b = 100, 8, 15
eps = rng.standard_normal((10_000, n))
x = x0 + (a*dt + b*np.sqrt(dt)*eps).cumsum(axis=1)   # Δx = aΔt + bε√Δt, added up
print(x[:, 62].mean(), x[:, 62].std())               # t = 0.25: ≈ 102, ≈ 7.5
print(x[:, -1].mean(), x[:, -1].std())               # t = 1:    ≈ 108, ≈ 15
```

> **Common pitfall.** Scaling b by T instead of √T. Over 3 months the st. dev. is 15 × √0.25 = 7.5, not 15 × 0.25 = 3.75. Drift scales with time; st. dev. scales with √time.
