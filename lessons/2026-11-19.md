### Recall

### Today's concept
*Hull: "The Black–Scholes–Merton Model" — "Implied Volatilities" and "Dividends" (European options; continuous yield from the stock index options chapter).*

**Implied volatility.** Take a market option price and find the σ that makes BSM reproduce it. There's no closed form, but the BSM price rises steadily with σ, so a simple search works (bisection: halve the interval each time).

*Example:* S₀ = 100, K = 105, r = 4%, T = 0.75. At σ = 25% the formula gives 7.79 (Day 21). If the market price is **9.00**, the implied vol must be higher: **28.5%**. At 6.00 it's **19.8%**; at 12.00 it's **37.2%**.

**Why traders care.**
- Implied vol is the market's forward-looking view of volatility, while historical vol (Day 18) looks backward.
- Traders **quote options in implied vol**, not dollars. "The 105 call is at 28.5" is easier to compare across strikes and maturities than a price.
- Indices of implied vol exist (e.g. the VIX for the S&P 500, the "fear index").
- If BSM were exactly right, every strike would give the same implied vol. In practice it doesn't: that's the *volatility smile*, coming in Module E.

**Dividends (European options).** On the ex-dividend date the stock price drops by roughly the dividend D. Hull's fix: the stock is "riskless dividends + a risky part", so
  **replace S₀ by S₀ − PV(dividends during the option's life)** and use the usual formula.

*Example:* a $2 dividend in 3 months: S₀* = 100 − 2e^{−0.04×0.25} = **98.02**.
- Call falls from 7.79 to **6.82**; put rises from 9.68 to **10.69**.
- That makes sense: dividends pull the expected future price down, which hurts calls and helps puts.

**Continuous dividend yield q** (e.g. a stock index): replace S₀ by S₀e^{−qT}. Equivalently, d₁ = [ln(S₀/K) + (r − q + σ²/2)T]/(σ√T) and c = S₀e^{−qT}N(d₁) − Ke^{−rT}N(d₂). With q = 2%: c = **7.05**.

**Code** (implied vol by bisection):
```python
from math import log, sqrt, exp, erf
N = lambda x: 0.5*(1 + erf(x/sqrt(2)))
def bs_call(S, K, r, sigma, T, q=0.0):
    d1 = (log(S/K) + (r - q + sigma**2/2)*T)/(sigma*sqrt(T)); d2 = d1 - sigma*sqrt(T)
    return S*exp(-q*T)*N(d1) - K*exp(-r*T)*N(d2)
def implied_vol(price, S, K, r, T, lo=1e-4, hi=3.0):
    for _ in range(100):                         # bisection: price increases with σ
        mid = (lo + hi)/2
        if bs_call(S, K, r, mid, T) > price: hi = mid
        else: lo = mid
    return mid
print(implied_vol(9.00, 100, 105, 0.04, 0.75))   # ≈ 0.2851
```

> **Common pitfall.** Treating implied vol as a forecast you can "check" against historical vol and call one of them wrong. They answer different questions: one is what the market is charging, the other is what happened. A gap between them is information (risk premia, expected events), not an error.
