### Today's concept
*Hull: "Binomial Trees" — "A One-Step Binomial Model and a No-Arbitrage Argument".*

**Two finance ideas first.**
- **Short selling:** borrow a share, sell it now, and buy it back later. You gain if the price falls. Holding "−0.5 shares" just means short half a share.
- **No-arbitrage:** there's no free lunch. If two portfolios always pay the same, they must cost the same today. A portfolio with a *certain* payoff must earn exactly the risk-free rate r.

**Set-up.** S₀ = 100. In 3 months (T = 0.25) the stock is either 110 or 90. r = 4% (continuously compounded), so e^{rT} = e^{0.01} = 1.01005. A call with strike K = 100 pays f_u = 10 (up) or f_d = 0 (down). What is it worth today?

**Build a riskless portfolio.** Hold **Δ shares** and **short 1 call**. At T it is worth:
- up: 110Δ − 10
- down: 90Δ − 0

Choose Δ so these are equal: 110Δ − 10 = 90Δ → **Δ = 0.5**. Then the portfolio is worth **45 in both states**: no risk at all.

**Price it.** A riskless 45 at T is worth 45e^{−0.01} = **44.552** today. The portfolio costs 0.5 × 100 − f, where f is the call price. So
  50 − f = 44.552 → **f = 5.448**,
which matches yesterday's p* answer (to rounding — yesterday used 1.01). In general (Hull's formula):

  **Δ = (f_u − f_d) / (S₀u − S₀d)**

**Why it must be 5.448.** Suppose the call trades at 6. Sell the call (+6), buy 0.5 shares (−50), and borrow 44.552 (+44.552). Your net cash today is **+0.552**. At T your shares-minus-call position is worth exactly 45 in both states, which repays the loan (44.552 × 1.01005 = 45). That's free money: an arbitrage. Traders would do this until the price fell to 5.448.

**The big surprise.** We never used the real probability of an up move. Whether p is 0.6 or 0.9, the price is 5.448. Hedging removes the risk, and with it any need to know the odds.

**Code:**
```python
import numpy as np
def one_step(S0, u, d, K, r, T):
    fu, fd = max(S0*u - K, 0), max(S0*d - K, 0)   # call payoffs up / down
    delta = (fu - fd) / (S0*u - S0*d)             # shares that make it riskless
    value_T = S0*u*delta - fu                      # portfolio value at T (same in both states)
    f = S0*delta - value_T*np.exp(-r*T)            # cost today = shares − PV(riskless value)
    return delta, f
print(one_step(100, 1.1, 0.9, 100, 0.04, 0.25))   # (0.5, 5.4478)
```

> **Common pitfall.** Pricing an option as "expected payoff, discounted" with the *real* probability (yesterday's 5.94). That ignores the fact that the option can be copied with shares and borrowing. The copying cost is the price, and that cost doesn't depend on p.
