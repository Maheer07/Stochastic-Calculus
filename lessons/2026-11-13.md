### Recall
Week 1, Day 4: in a binomial tree, hold **Δ shares and short 1 option**. Δ makes the portfolio riskless, so it must earn r, and that pins down the option price with no need for the real probability. Black–Scholes–Merton is **exactly the same idea in continuous time**, with Itô's lemma (Day 14) doing the work of the tree.

### Today's concept
*Hull: "The Black–Scholes–Merton Model" — "Concepts Underlying the Black–Scholes–Merton Differential Equation", "Assumptions" and "Derivation of the Black–Scholes–Merton Differential Equation" (first half).*

**Hull's assumptions (short version).** The stock follows dS = μS dt + σS dz with constant μ and σ; short selling is allowed; there are no transaction costs, taxes or dividends; trading is continuous; there are no riskless arbitrage opportunities; and the risk-free rate r is constant.

**Step 1 — Two processes, one source of risk.** Let f(S, t) be the price of any derivative on S (a call, a put…). Itô's lemma gives
  df = ( ∂f/∂S μS + ∂f/∂t + ½ ∂²f/∂S² σ²S² ) dt + ∂f/∂S σS dz.
In discrete form over a short Δt:
  ΔS = μS Δt + σS Δz
  Δf = ( ∂f/∂S μS + ∂f/∂t + ½ ∂²f/∂S² σ²S² ) Δt + ∂f/∂S σS Δz
**The same Δz drives both.** That's the key fact: the risk in f and the risk in S are perfectly linked over a short interval.

**Step 2 — Build the hedge.** Hold
  **−1 derivative and +∂f/∂S shares**.
Its value is Π = −f + (∂f/∂S)S. Over Δt (holding ∂f/∂S fixed during the step):
  ΔΠ = −Δf + (∂f/∂S) ΔS.

**Step 3 — Substitute and watch the risk vanish.** The Δz terms are −(∂f/∂S)σS Δz from −Δf and +(∂f/∂S)σS Δz from the shares, so they **cancel exactly**. The μ terms (−∂f/∂S μS Δt and +∂f/∂S μS Δt) **also cancel**. What's left is
  **ΔΠ = ( −∂f/∂t − ½ ∂²f/∂S² σ²S² ) Δt**.
No Δz means no risk over this instant, and no μ means investors' risk preferences have dropped out, just as the real probability p did in the tree. (The Δ in the tree is the ∂f/∂S here.)

On Monday (part 2): a riskless portfolio must earn r, so ΔΠ = rΠΔt. That one line turns today's result into the BSM equation.

**Code** (see the hedge work with a "derivative" we can differentiate by hand: f = S², so ∂f/∂S = 2S):
```python
import numpy as np
S, mu, sigma, dt = 100.0, 0.08, 0.2, 1/252
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
eps = rng.standard_normal(100_000)
dS = S*(mu*dt + sigma*np.sqrt(dt)*eps)              # one day's stock move
df = (S + dS)**2 - S**2                             # change in f = S²
dPi = -df + 2*S*dS                                  # short 1 f, long ∂f/∂S = 2S shares
print(df.std(), dPi.std())    # ≈ 253 vs ≈ 2.3: the hedge removes ~99% of the risk
print(dPi.mean(), -sigma**2 * S**2 * dt)   # ≈ −1.60 vs −1.587 = (−∂f/∂t − ½·f_SS·σ²S²)Δt
```

> **Common pitfall.** Thinking the hedge ratio is fixed. ∂f/∂S changes as S and t change (Day 6's Δ through the tree), so the hedge is riskless only *instantaneously* and must be rebalanced continuously. With discrete rebalancing some risk leaks back in. Q3 shows how much.

## Solutions

**Q1: A.** The Δz terms cancel by construction (that's what ∂f/∂S shares are for), and the μ terms cancel with them. B is wrong: Δt terms survive (−∂f/∂t − ½ f_SS σ²S²). C: σ survives inside the ½ f_SS σ²S² term. D: ∂f/∂t survives.

**Q2.** (a) ∂f/∂S = **2S**, ∂²f/∂S² = **2**, ∂f/∂t = **0**. (b) ΔΠ = (0 − ½ · 2 · 0.04 · 10,000)/252 = −400/252 = **−1.587**. (c) −[(S + ΔS)² − S²] + 2SΔS = −(2SΔS + ΔS²) + 2SΔS = **−(ΔS)²** ✓. Its mean is −E[(ΔS)²] ≈ −σ²S²Δt, by Day 13's (Δz)² ≈ Δt. It's not *exactly* constant at finite Δt, but its spread is far smaller than Δf's.

**Q3.** Seed 42, daily: st. dev. Δf ≈ **253**, ΔΠ ≈ **2.28**, a **~111×** reduction; mean ΔΠ ≈ **−1.600** vs **−1.587** (the tiny gap is a μ²Δt² term). Weekly: Δf ≈ **558**, ΔΠ ≈ **11.05**, only **~50×**; mean ≈ **−7.77** vs **−7.69**. The leftover −(ΔS)² has st. dev. of order σ²S²Δt, which grows linearly with Δt, while Δf's grows only like √Δt. Hedging less often leaves proportionally more risk. Continuous rebalancing (Δt → 0) is what makes the portfolio truly riskless.
