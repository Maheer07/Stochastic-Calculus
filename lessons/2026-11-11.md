### Today's concept
*Hull: "The Black–Scholes–Merton Model" — "The Distribution of the Rate of Return" and "The Expected Return".*

**The realized rate of return.** Define x as the continuously compounded return per year actually earned between 0 and T:
  S_T = S₀e^{xT}, so **x = (1/T) ln(S_T/S₀)**.
Dividing the lognormal result by T:
  **x ~ φ( μ − σ²/2 , σ²/T )**.
- The mean is μ − σ²/2, *not* μ.
- The st. dev. σ/√T *shrinks* with T. Over long horizons the annualized return becomes more predictable, even though the price itself gets less predictable (Day 9's √T).

*Example* (μ = 0.08, σ = 0.2, T = 4 years): x ~ φ(0.06, 0.01), st. dev. 10%. A 95% range for the average annual return is 6% ± 19.6%, i.e. **−13.6% to +25.6%**.

**Two "expected returns".**
- **μ**: the expected return over a *very short* period, E[ΔS/S] = μΔt. It's the arithmetic average of returns, and what goes into the stock model and into E[S_T] = S₀e^{μT}.
- **μ − σ²/2**: the expected *continuously compounded* return actually achieved over a period, i.e. the growth rate of the typical (median) path. It's the geometric average.

**The gap is σ²/2 every time.** Hull's mutual-fund illustration, with our own numbers: annual returns of +20%, −10%, +15%, +5%, −5%.
- Arithmetic average: (20 − 10 + 15 + 5 − 5)/5 = **5.0%**.
- What you actually earned: (1.20 × 0.90 × 1.15 × 1.05 × 0.95)^{1/5} − 1 = **4.4%** a year.

The arithmetic mean flatters you. The difference grows with volatility. This is Day 2's +10%/−10% → −1% story, and Itô's −σ²/2, seen in real fund data.

**Code** (5 years of annual returns; compare the two averages):
```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
mu, sigma, T, M = 0.08, 0.2, 5, 100_000
g = np.exp((mu - sigma**2/2) + sigma*rng.standard_normal((M, T)))  # yearly growth factors (lognormal)
x = np.log(g.prod(axis=1)) / T                                     # realized cont. compounded return
print(x.mean(), x.std())          # ≈ 0.060 = μ − σ²/2, ≈ 0.089 = σ/√5
print((g - 1).mean())             # ≈ 0.083 = e^μ − 1: the arithmetic average annual return
print(np.exp(np.log(g).mean()) - 1)   # ≈ 0.062 = e^(μ−σ²/2) − 1: the geometric average
```

> **Common pitfall.** Quoting a fund's or stock's arithmetic average return as what an investor "earns per year". Compounded, the investor earns roughly the arithmetic mean minus σ²/2. For σ = 30% that's a 4.5% a year gap.
