### Today's concept
*Hull: "The Greek Letters" — "Illustration", "Naked and Covered Positions", "A Stop-Loss Strategy" and "Delta Hedging" (with the table of hedge performance by rebalancing frequency).*

**The set-up.** A bank sells a 9-month call (S₀ = 100, K = 105, r = 4%, σ = 25%) for its BSM value, **7.79**. The real stock drifts at μ = 8%. How should it manage the risk?

**Option 1 — naked position (do nothing).** Fine if the stock ends below 105. If the stock ends at 140, the bank pays 35. There's huge risk either way.

**Option 2 — covered position (buy one share now).** Fine if the stock rises. If it falls to 70, the share loses 30. That's still huge risk.

**Option 3 — stop-loss.** Hold one share whenever S > K, and none when S < K: buy at 105 on the way up and sell at 105 on the way down. It *sounds* perfect: you end with the share exactly when you need it. But in practice you buy slightly above K and sell slightly below. Because of Day 10's jagged paths, the price crosses K many times, so the small losses add up. Simulated (20,000 paths), the hedging error has st. dev. **≈ 0.89 × the option price**, whether you check weekly or daily. More frequent checking doesn't fix it.

**Option 4 — delta hedging.** Hold **Δ = N(d₁)** shares and rebalance as Δ changes:
- Today Δ = 0.5086, so the bank buys **0.509 shares per call sold**.
- If the stock is at 102 a week later, Δ rises to **0.543**: buy 0.034 more shares.
- If it falls, sell some.

Every rebalance is "buy high, sell low" in small amounts. Over the option's life these trades cost on average exactly the BSM price. That's what the price *is*.

**How well does it work?** Simulated with real-world μ = 8%, using Hull's measure st. dev.(PV of hedging P&L) / option price:

| Rebalance every | 4 weeks | 1 week | 1 day | ¼ day | Stop-loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Performance | 0.31 | 0.16 | 0.07 | 0.04 | ≈ 0.89 |

Each 4× increase in frequency halves the error: error ∝ 1/√(frequency), Day 9's √ again. The mean P&L is ≈ 0 in every case, even though μ = 8% ≠ r. The hedge doesn't care about μ.

**Code** (weekly delta hedge, numpy only):
```python
import numpy as np
from math import erf
N = np.vectorize(lambda x: 0.5*(1 + erf(x/np.sqrt(2))))       # normal CDF, works on arrays
S0, K, r, sigma, T, mu = 100, 105, 0.04, 0.25, 0.75, 0.08
def delta(S, tau): return N((np.log(S/K) + (r + sigma**2/2)*tau)/(sigma*np.sqrt(tau)))
def hedge_error(n, M=20_000, seed=42):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed); dt = T/n
    S = np.full(M, float(S0)); pos = delta(S, T)
    cash = 7.7865 - pos*S                       # sold the call for its BSM price, bought Δ shares
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        S = S*np.exp((mu - sigma**2/2)*dt + sigma*np.sqrt(dt)*rng.standard_normal(M))  # real world
        cash *= np.exp(r*dt)                    # cash earns interest
        new = delta(S, T - i*dt) if i < n else (S > K).astype(float)
        cash -= (new - pos)*S; pos = new        # rebalance to the new delta
    pnl = (cash + pos*S - np.maximum(S - K, 0))*np.exp(-r*T)   # PV of final P&L
    return pnl.mean(), pnl.std()/7.7865         # Hull's performance measure
print(hedge_error(39))                          # weekly: mean ≈ 0, ratio ≈ 0.16
```

> **Common pitfall.** Thinking "delta-hedged" means "no risk". It means no risk from *small* moves *between* rebalances, and only if the model is right. Discrete rebalancing, jumps and the wrong σ all leave residual P&L. Tomorrow's gamma measures how fast that error builds up.

## Solutions

**Q1: A (it halves).** Error ∝ 1/√(frequency), so 4× more often gives ½ the error (0.31 → 0.16 in the table). B would need error ∝ 1/frequency.

**Q2.** (a) Buy 10,000 × 0.5086 = **5,086 shares** for ≈ $508,600. You received 10,000 × 7.79 = $77,900, so you borrow ≈ **$430,700**. (b) New position 10,000 × 0.543 = 5,430 shares: **buy ≈ 344 more** at 102. (c) The price crosses K many times, and each round trip buys a bit above K and sells a bit below. Those small losses add up and don't shrink with more frequent checking, because a Wiener path crosses any level infinitely often near each crossing (Day 10).

**Q3.** Seed 42, 20,000 paths: n = 9: mean ≈ **−0.035**, ratio ≈ **0.31**. n = 39: mean ≈ **−0.012**, ratio ≈ **0.16**. n = 189: mean ≈ **−0.002**, ratio ≈ **0.07**. The ratio falls like 1/√n: ×0.50 for 9 → 39, and ×0.46 for 39 → 189 (1/√4.8 ≈ 0.46). The mean is near 0 in every case: delta hedging earns back the BSM price on average, whatever μ is.
