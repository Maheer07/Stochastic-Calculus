### Recall
Itô's lemma for a function G(S, t) of a stock:
  dG = (∂G/∂S μS + ∂G/∂t + ½ ∂²G/∂S² σ²S²) dt + ∂G/∂S σS dz.
Recipe: (1) find the three partial derivatives, (2) plug them in, (3) tidy up. Today we use it on two of Hull's own examples.

### Today's concept
*Hull: "Wiener Processes and Itô's Lemma" — "Application to Forward Contracts" and "Lognormal Property".*

**Application 1: the forward price.** A forward contract fixes today a price F to buy the stock at time T. For a non-dividend stock, no-arbitrage gives **F = S e^{r(T−t)}** (Hull's *Determination of Forward and Futures Prices* chapter). Why: buying the stock now and borrowing S costs nothing today and delivers the stock at T for Se^{r(T−t)}, so a different F would be an arbitrage. How does F move as S moves?
1. Partials: ∂F/∂S = e^{r(T−t)}, ∂²F/∂S² = **0** (F is a straight line in S), ∂F/∂t = −rSe^{r(T−t)}.
2. Plug in: dF = [e^{r(T−t)}μS − rSe^{r(T−t)} + 0] dt + e^{r(T−t)}σS dz.
3. Tidy (Se^{r(T−t)} = F): **dF = (μ − r)F dt + σF dz**.

So the forward price is also a geometric Brownian motion, with the **same volatility σ** as the stock but drift **μ − r**. Since ∂²F/∂S² = 0, there's no Itô correction here: the ordinary chain rule would have given the same answer. Itô only "bites" when G is curved in S.

**Application 2: the lognormal property, in full.** On Friday: G = ln S gives **d(ln S) = (μ − σ²/2) dt + σ dz**. The drift and volatility are constants, so ln S is a generalized Wiener process (Day 11), and
  **ln S_T ~ φ[ ln S₀ + (μ − σ²/2)T , σ²T ]**.

*Worked example* (S₀ = 100, μ = 0.08, σ = 0.2, T = 6 months):
- mean of ln S_T = ln 100 + (0.08 − 0.02)(0.5) = 4.6052 + 0.03 = **4.6352**
- variance 0.04 × 0.5 = **0.02**; st. dev. **0.1414**
- 95% confidence interval: e^{4.6352 ± 1.96 × 0.1414} = **78.10 to 135.96**
- P(S_T > 110) = 1 − N((ln 110 − 4.6352)/0.1414) = 1 − N(0.462) = **32.2%**

That's Hull's lognormal example with our numbers, and it's exactly where Module C picks up on Wednesday.

**Code** (check the interval and the probability by simulating dS directly):
```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
dt = 1/252; S = np.full(100_000, 100.0)
for _ in range(126):                                   # 6 months of daily steps
    S = S * (1 + 0.08*dt + 0.2*np.sqrt(dt)*rng.standard_normal(100_000))
print(np.percentile(S, [2.5, 97.5]))                   # ≈ [78.1, 135.9] — matches 78.10 to 135.96
print((S > 110).mean())                                # ≈ 0.323 — matches 32.2%
```

> **Common pitfall.** Building a confidence interval for S_T as "mean ± 1.96 st. dev." directly on S. S_T is lognormal (skewed), so you build the interval on **ln S_T** (which *is* normal) and then exponentiate. The result isn't symmetric around the mean: 78.10 is 25 below 103, but 135.96 is 33 above.
