### Today's concept
*Hull: "Interest Rates" — "Continuous Compounding"; "The Black–Scholes–Merton Model" — "Lognormal Property of Stock Prices" and "The Distribution of the Rate of Return".*

**Two tools.** e ≈ 2.718. eˣ is "grow continuously at rate x" (e^0.05 ≈ 1.0513). ln x undoes it: ln(eˣ) = x. The key rule is **ln(a·b) = ln a + ln b**: logs turn multiplication into addition.

**Continuous compounding.** $100 at 5% a year: compounded annually gives 105.00, monthly 100(1 + 0.05/12)¹² = 105.12, and compounded *continuously* 100·e^0.05 = 105.13. Hull uses continuous compounding throughout: growth over T years is e^{rT}, and discounting is multiplying by e^{−rT}.

**Simple vs log returns.** A stock goes +10% then −10%. The simple returns "add" to 0%, but you actually end at 100 × 1.1 × 0.9 = 99, a loss of 1%. Log returns *do* add: ln 1.1 + ln 0.9 = 0.0953 − 0.1054 = −0.0101, and e^−0.0101 = 0.99 ✓.

**From products to the lognormal.** ln(S_T/S₀) is the sum of many small, independent log returns. A sum of many small independent pieces is approximately normal (the central limit theorem). So **ln S_T is normal, and S_T is lognormal**. Hull's result for a stock with expected return μ and volatility σ:

  ln S_T ~ φ[ ln S₀ + (μ − σ²/2)T , σ²T ]

(φ[m, v] = normal with mean m and variance v.) Consequences:
- E[S_T] = S₀e^{μT}
- Var(S_T) = S₀²e^{2μT}(e^{σ²T} − 1)
- median of S_T = S₀e^{(μ − σ²/2)T}, which is **below** the mean.

The −σ²/2 is *volatility drag*: the +10%/−10% effect, averaged over lots of ups and downs.

**Worked example** (S₀ = 100, μ = 0.08, σ = 0.2, T = 1):
- ln S_T has mean ln 100 + (0.08 − 0.02) = 4.6652 and standard deviation 0.2.
- 95% interval: e^{4.6652 ± 1.96×0.2} → **71.75 to 157.15**.
- E[S_T] = 100e^0.08 = **108.33**; s.d. of S_T = √(10⁴ · e^0.16 · (e^0.04 − 1)) = **21.88**; median = 100e^0.06 = **106.18**.

**Code** (simulate S_T directly from the formula):
```python
import numpy as np
S0, mu, sigma, T = 100, 0.08, 0.2, 1.0
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)              # random number generator (fixed seed = repeatable)
eps = rng.standard_normal(100_000)           # 100,000 draws of ε ~ N(0, 1)
ST = S0 * np.exp((mu - 0.5*sigma**2)*T + sigma*np.sqrt(T)*eps)   # lognormal S_T
print(ST.mean(), ST.std(), np.median(ST))    # ≈ 108.3, 21.9, 106.2
print((ST < 100).mean())                     # fraction of paths that lose money ≈ 0.38
```

> **Common pitfall.** Thinking the "typical" outcome is S₀e^{μT}. That's the *mean*, pulled up by a few big winners. The *median* (the typical path) is S₀e^{(μ − σ²/2)T}. The gap grows with σ.

**Q3.** With σ = 0.4 (seed 42 run): mean ≈ **108.5** (theory 108.33 — unchanged, because E[S_T] = S₀e^{μT} doesn't depend on σ); median ≈ **100.1** (theory 100e^{0.08 − 0.08} = 100.00, down from 106.18); fraction below 100 ≈ **0.50** (theory N(0) = 0.5, up from N(−0.3) ≈ 0.38). Higher volatility keeps the average the same but makes the typical outcome worse, because a few huge winners prop up the mean. That's volatility drag.
