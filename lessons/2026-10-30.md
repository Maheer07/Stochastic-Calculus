### Today's concept
*Hull: "Wiener Processes and Itô's Lemma" — "The Markov Property" and "Continuous-Time Stochastic Processes".*

**Stochastic process.** A variable whose value changes over time in an uncertain way (a stock price, an interest rate). It can change at discrete times or continuously. Hull works in *continuous time*, and he treats the price itself as a continuous variable.

**The Markov property.** A process is **Markov** if only its *current* value matters for predicting the future. How it got there is irrelevant. If IBM is at $100 today, yesterday's $95 or last week's $110 adds nothing to the forecast.
- Our binomial tree is Markov: the next move depends only on the current node.
- Hull links this to **weak-form market efficiency**: if past prices helped predict future prices, traders would exploit it until the pattern vanished. So stock price models assume Markov.

**Variances add.** Suppose x is Markov and its change over 1 year is φ(0, 1) (normal, mean 0, variance 1). Because each year's change is independent of the past:
- Over **2 years**: φ(0, 1) + φ(0, 1) = **φ(0, 2)**, st. dev. √2 = 1.41 (not 2!)
- Over **6 months**: two equal halves must add to variance 1, so each is **φ(0, 0.5)**, st. dev. 0.71
- Over **Δt years**: **φ(0, Δt)**, st. dev. **√Δt**

**Uncertainty grows with the square root of time.** That's exactly yesterday's √Δt step size, now explained.

**Practical payoff — scaling volatility.** With σ = 20% a year (in log terms):
- 1 day (1/252 of a trading year): 0.2 × √(1/252) = **1.26%**
- 1 week: 0.2 × √(1/52) = **2.77%**
- 1 month: 0.2 × √(1/12) = **5.77%**

Going the other way, annual vol = daily vol × √252.

**Code** (check √t scaling and "no memory"):
```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 252; dt = 1/n                                    # daily steps over 1 year
dz = rng.standard_normal((10_000, n)) * np.sqrt(dt)  # each step ~ φ(0, Δt)
z = dz.cumsum(axis=1)                                # running total = the process
for t in (0.25, 0.5, 1.0):
    i = int(round(t*n)) - 1
    print(t, z[:, i].std(), np.sqrt(t))              # st. dev. ≈ √t
first_half = z[:, 125]; second_half = z[:, -1] - z[:, 125]
print(np.corrcoef(first_half, second_half)[0, 1])    # ≈ 0: the past doesn't predict the future
```

> **Common pitfall.** Adding standard deviations instead of variances. Two independent years at 20% vol each give 0.2 × √2 = **28.3%** over two years, not 40%. Square, add, then square-root.
