### Today's concept
*Hull: "Binomial Trees" — "Risk-Neutral Valuation" and "Real World vs. Risk-Neutral World".*

**The algebra (3 lines).** Friday's recipe in symbols: the option price is
  f = S₀Δ − (S₀uΔ − f_u)e^{−rT}, with Δ = (f_u − f_d)/(S₀u − S₀d).
Substitute Δ and tidy up (Hull does this step by step), and you get
  **f = e^{−rT} [ p f_u + (1 − p) f_d ]**, where **p = (e^{rT} − d)/(u − d)**.
The option price is the expected payoff using p, discounted at r.

**What p means.** Under p, the stock's expected price is p·S₀u + (1 − p)·S₀d = S₀e^{rT}. Check with our tree: 90 + 20 × 0.5503 = 101.005 = 100e^{0.01} ✓. So under p the stock earns exactly the risk-free rate. A world where every asset is expected to earn r is a **risk-neutral world**.

**Risk-neutral valuation (Hull's three steps):**
1. Assume every asset's expected return is the risk-free rate r.
2. Compute the derivative's expected payoff in that world.
3. Discount it at r.

**Why μ drops out.** The hedge in Friday's lesson used only u, d and r. The real chance of an up move (and so μ) never entered. Investors' attitudes to risk change μ and p *together* in the real world, but they can't change the copying cost.

**Real world vs risk-neutral world.** Our tree's real p = 0.6 means the stock grows 2% per quarter on average, so μ ≈ 4 × ln 1.02 = **7.9%**, close to our running μ = 0.08. In the real world:
- the call's expected payoff is 0.6 × 10 = 6;
- to get the true price 5.448, you'd have to discount at q where 6e^{−0.25q} = 5.448, giving **q = 38.6%** a year.

The option is a leveraged bet on the stock, so it needs a much higher discount rate. That rate is hard to know in advance, and it changes with S and t. Risk-neutral valuation sidesteps it entirely.

**Code:**
```python
import numpy as np
def rn_price(S0, u, d, K, r, T, kind="call"):
    p = (np.exp(r*T) - d) / (u - d)                 # risk-neutral probability
    payoff = lambda S: max(S - K, 0) if kind == "call" else max(K - S, 0)
    return p, np.exp(-r*T) * (p*payoff(S0*u) + (1-p)*payoff(S0*d))   # discounted E*[payoff]
print(rn_price(100, 1.1, 0.9, 100, 0.04, 0.25))          # (0.5503, 5.4478) — same as Friday
print(rn_price(100, 1.1, 0.9, 100, 0.04, 0.25, "put"))   # (0.5503, 4.4527)
```

> **Common pitfall.** Treating p as a forecast — "the risk-neutral p is 0.55, so the stock goes up 55% of the time". p is a pricing device, not a prediction. The real probability could be 0.6 or 0.9. Use real probabilities for forecasting and risk management, and risk-neutral ones for pricing.

**Q3.**
| real p | stock μ | option's real-world q |
|---|---|---|
| 0.6 | 7.9% | 38.6% |
| 0.7 | 15.7% | 100.3% |
| 0.8 | 23.3% | 153.7% |
