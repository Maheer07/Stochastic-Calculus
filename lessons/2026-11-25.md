### Recall
Delta covers small moves in S. Gamma covers how quickly that hedge goes stale. Theta is the cost of time passing. But BSM also *assumed* σ and r are constant, and in reality both move. Today: the Greeks for those two inputs, and how a desk neutralises several Greeks at once.

### Today's concept
*Hull: "The Greek Letters" — "Vega", "Rho", and the examples on making a portfolio gamma-neutral and gamma- and vega-neutral.*

**Vega (ν) = ∂Π/∂σ.** Sensitivity to volatility.
  **ν = S√T N′(d₁)** (calls and puts alike; always positive for a long option).
Our call: ν = 100 × √0.75 × 0.3988 = **34.54** per unit of σ, i.e. **+0.345 per 1 vol point**. Check: repricing at σ = 26% raises c by 0.345 ✓.

**Rho (ρ) = ∂Π/∂r.** Sensitivity to the interest rate.
  **ρ_call = KTe^{−rT}N(d₂)**, which is **32.30** here (+0.32 per 1% rise in r). Rho is usually the least important Greek for short-dated equity options.

**Why you need *options* to hedge gamma and vega.** A stock position has Δ = 1 but **Γ = 0 and ν = 0**. Shares can fix delta, but nothing else. To change gamma or vega you must trade other options.

**Worked example — a short book.** We're short 10,000 of our call (K = 105, T = 0.75):
- Δ = −5,086, **Γ = −184.2**, **ν = −345,415** (−3,454 per vol point).
- Available to trade: option A (K = 100, T = 0.25; Δ = 0.557, Γ = 0.0316, ν = 19.75) and option B (K = 110, T = 1.0; Δ = 0.462, Γ = 0.0159, ν = 39.71).

*Step 1 — gamma-neutral with A alone (Hull's recipe):* buy w = 184.2/0.0316 = **5,831 A**. Delta becomes −5,086 + 5,831 × 0.557 = −1,840, so **buy 1,840 shares**. Now Δ = Γ = 0, but ν is still −230,276. We remain badly exposed to a rise in volatility.

*Step 2 — gamma- and vega-neutral with A and B:* solve two equations in two unknowns:
  0.0316·w_A + 0.0159·w_B = 184.2
  19.75·w_A + 39.71·w_B = 345,415
⇒ **w_A ≈ 1,944, w_B ≈ 7,732**. The new delta is −434, so **buy 434 shares**. Now Δ = Γ = ν = 0.

**The rule:** each Greek you want to neutralise (beyond delta) needs one more traded option. Delta is always fixed last, with shares, because shares don't disturb gamma or vega.

**Code** (Greeks and the 2×2 hedge):
```python
import numpy as np
from math import log, sqrt, exp, erf, pi
N  = lambda x: 0.5*(1 + erf(x/sqrt(2))); Nd = lambda x: exp(-x*x/2)/sqrt(2*pi)
S, r, sigma = 100, 0.04, 0.25
def greeks(K, T):                                   # returns delta, gamma, vega of a call
    d1 = (log(S/K) + (r + sigma**2/2)*T)/(sigma*sqrt(T))
    return np.array([N(d1), Nd(d1)/(S*sigma*sqrt(T)), S*sqrt(T)*Nd(d1)])
book = -10_000*greeks(105, 0.75)                    # [Δ, Γ, ν] of the short book
A, B = greeks(100, 0.25), greeks(110, 1.0)
w = np.linalg.solve([[A[1], B[1]], [A[2], B[2]]], [-book[1], -book[2]])   # kill Γ and ν
shares = -(book[0] + w[0]*A[0] + w[1]*B[0])          # then kill Δ with stock
print(w, shares)                                    # ≈ [1944, 7732], ≈ 434
```

> **Common pitfall.** Making a book delta-neutral with shares and calling it "hedged". It can still have huge gamma (big-move risk) and vega (volatility risk). Shares can't touch either. Step 1 above was delta- *and* gamma-neutral but still carried −230,000 of vega.
